Representative Elementary Volume (REV) Analysis (lbpm_rev_analysis)¶

The degree of heterogeneity observed in digital rocks is an important factor in characterizing porous media. Advances in the resolution and sample size achievable with X-ray microtomography have made it increasingly feasible to digitally investigate larger volumes that capture the intrinsic heterogeneities of porous structures. These developments enable the identification of representative elementary volumes (REVs), beyond which the influence of local heterogeneities becomes negligible and specific effective properties of the porous medium can be reliably characterized.

The lbpm_rev_analysis routine provides an automated REV analysis for a range of geometrical and hydrodynamic properties, as summarized in Table 1. By combining deterministic and statistical approaches, the routine enables a comprehensive characterization of these properties, providing valuable insights for assessing the representativeness of microtomography images and estimating effective sample volumes for subsequent numerical analyses.

Methodology¶

The implemented REV routine combines deterministic and statistical approaches into a hybrid methodology, as illustrated in the workflow diagram presented in Fig. 5. The deterministic stage provides an initial estimate of the REV size at a relatively low computational cost. This estimate is subsequently used to initialize the statistical stage, which evaluates the spatial variability of the property over randomly distributed subvolumes. Both deterministic and statistical analyses employ a default convergence threshold of \(\gamma = 0.1\) for their respective criteria. The final REV is accepted only when the statistical convergence conditions are satisfied. For a detailed explanation of the steps illustrated in Fig. 5, expand the toggle sections below for the deterministic and statistical analyses.

Deterministic Analysis¶

Click here to open: Deterministic Analysis

The deterministic approach evaluates a property of interest within a subvolume centered in the computational domain. The analysis is repeated for progressively larger subvolumes until the measured property becomes approximately independent of the subvolume edge length. The smallest subvolume satisfying the prescribed convergence criteria is identified as the deterministic REV.

The main advantage of this approach is its relatively low computational cost, as only one subvolume is evaluated for each edge length. However, the results may be sensitive to local fluctuations, including maxima, minima, and inflection points, potentially leading to false convergence.

The deterministic procedure, illustrated in the upper part of the workflow diagram, consists of the following steps:

  1. Initialize the subvolume size: The initial edge length is defined as twice the average pore diameter,

    (110)¶\[\ell_0 = 2\overline{d}_p.\]

  2. Evaluate the domain-size constraint: Before each evaluation, the subvolume dimensions are checked against the available computational domain. If the prescribed maximum size is exceeded before convergence, no deterministic REV is identified.

  3. Measure the property: The property of interest, \(\eta_i\), is evaluated within the centered subvolume.

  4. Compute the convergence criteria: Two complementary metrics are calculated to quantify the variation of the measured property with increasing subvolume size.

    The first metric is the relative error (RE), defined following as

    (111)¶\[RE_i = \left| \frac{\eta_{i+1}-\eta_{i-1}} {\eta_{i+1}+\eta_{i-1}} \frac{V_{i-1}}{V_{i+1}} \right| < \gamma,\]

    where \(RE_i\) is the relative error at iteration \(i\), \(\eta_i\) represents the measured property in the \(i\)-th subvolume, and \(V_i\) denotes its corresponding volume. This metric quantifies the local variation of the property as the evaluated subvolume increases in size.

    The second metric is the convergence criterion (CC), introduced by :

    (112)¶\[CC_i = \frac{ \sigma(\eta_{i-m+1},\ldots,\eta_i) }{ \overline{\eta}(\eta_{i-m+1},\ldots,\eta_i) } < \gamma,\]

    where \(\sigma\) denotes the standard deviation, \(\overline{\eta}\) represents the arithmetic mean, and \(m\) is the number of consecutive measurements considered. By evaluating the dispersion of the most recent measurements, this metric reduces the likelihood of false convergence associated with local fluctuations. The default value is \(m=10\).

  5. Assess convergence and update the subvolume size: The deterministic REV is identified when both convergence criteria satisfy the prescribed tolerance. Otherwise, the subvolume edge length is increased by 6%, according to

    (113)¶\[\ell_{i+1}=1.06\ell_i,\]

    and the procedure is repeated until convergence is achieved or the maximum admissible subvolume size is reached.


Statistical Analysis¶

Click here to open: Statistical Analysis

The statistical approach evaluates the spatial variability of a property by sampling multiple subvolumes distributed throughout the computational domain. For a given edge length, the property is measured in a set of randomly selected subvolumes, and the dispersion of the resulting measurements is assessed.

If the dispersion exceeds the prescribed tolerance, the subvolume size is increased and the procedure is repeated until the measurements become sufficiently consistent.

Compared with the deterministic approach, statistical analysis is computationally more expensive because multiple subvolumes must be evaluated at each edge length. Nevertheless, it provides a more robust assessment of spatial heterogeneity and reduces the risk of identifying false convergence caused by localized features.

An important limitation arises as the subvolume size approaches the dimensions of the computational domain. In this situation, increasing overlap between sampled subvolumes can artificially reduce the measured dispersion, potentially leading to an overestimation of convergence.

The statistical procedure, represented in the lower part of the workflow diagram, follows these steps:

  1. Initialize the statistical analysis: The edge length corresponding to the deterministic REV is adopted as the initial subvolume size, avoiding the repeated evaluation of smaller volumes that have already been assessed.

  2. Select and evaluate random subvolumes: For the prescribed edge length, \(N\) subvolumes are randomly distributed within the computational domain, and the property of interest is evaluated independently in each subvolume.

  3. Evaluate statistical convergence: The dispersion of the measurements is quantified using the coefficient of variation (CV), following :

    (114)¶\[CV = \frac{\sigma(\eta)}{\overline{\eta}} < \gamma,\]

    where \(\sigma(\eta)\) is the standard deviation of the property measurements and \(\overline{\eta}\) is their arithmetic mean. Normalization by the mean provides a dimensionless measure of relative dispersion.

    The number of sampled subvolumes is determined adaptively by monitoring the variation of the CV as additional subvolumes are included. Sampling continues until the estimated dispersion stabilizes, ensuring that the statistical measure is sufficiently representative of the selected subvolume size.

  4. Determine the statistical REV: Once the CV has stabilized, its value is compared with the prescribed convergence tolerance. If the criterion is satisfied, the current edge length is identified as the statistical REV. Otherwise, the subvolume edge length is increased by 6%, and the statistical evaluation is repeated. If the admissible domain size is exceeded before convergence, no statistical REV is identified.

Model parameters¶

REV {
   Geometrical = True
   Hydraulic = True
   gamma = 0.1
   SizeStep = 0.06
   CCInterval = 10
   StatSamples = 50
}

Geometrical and Hydraulic are boolean settings read into the REV class. Both default to True when omitted and accept True or False.
